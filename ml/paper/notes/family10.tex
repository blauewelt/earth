\begin{abstract}
Family 10 is the programme's storage contract for data of mixed granularity.
Nothing is resampled onto a common grid when it is stored: every source keeps
its own resolution and cadence, and the token a model reads carries the
measurement's \emph{footprint} --- how much area and how much time it averaged
--- beside its offset from the place and pentad being asked about. One registry
file, \code{tensors/family10/family10.json} on the public Hugging Face dataset
repository \code{chfrank/earth-tensors}, lists every group of the family and
says which of three storage tiers it belongs to: \textbf{G}, gridded dense
fields held at their native grid and read by a byte range (the four channel
groups of family 7.2, referenced rather than copied); \textbf{P}, points,
profiles and tracks held as columns sorted by five-day bin with a
compressed-sparse-row index and read by a $k$-nearest search; and \textbf{T},
tiles, which is designed and not built. Experiment E-079 built the four tier-P
stores: \code{gdp} (the Global Drifter Program's six-hourly surface drifters,
48,480,798 rows), \code{gtmba} (the tropical moored arrays TAO/TRITON, PIRATA
and RAMA, one row per mooring per day, 1,001,282 rows), \code{socat} (the
Surface Ocean CO\textsubscript{2} Atlas, 41,830,675 underway ship and mooring
measurements) and \code{slatrack} (Copernicus Marine's reprocessed along-track
sea-level anomaly, 2,030,800,150 samples from 29 altimeter mission datasets).
All four were published and then independently verified from the bytes on
2026-09-14, for \$0.55. This note states what the registry says, what a store
is, what each store contains, how the builds ran, how to read one, and what the
layout still cannot promise. A final section is a self-contained handover for an
external agent that reads the public dataset and never writes to it.
\end{abstract}

\section{Purpose: four prediction goals, served by measurements}

The programme tracks four prediction goals: the El Ni\~no--Southern Oscillation
(the tropical Pacific's warm-and-cold swing, read out as the Ni\~no-3.4
sea-surface temperature index), the ocean carbon sink (how much carbon dioxide
the sea surface absorbs), the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation or
AMOC (the northward heat-carrying flow of the Atlantic, read out as transport at
26.5$^\circ$\,N), and sea-surface temperature itself. Everything the programme
had fed a model before family 10 was a \emph{gridded product} --- a field
someone else had already interpolated onto a regular grid. Family 10's four
tier-P stores are the measurements themselves.

The design argument is in the family-10 design note (E-078) and is three
measurements rather than three preferences. A coarse value copied into fine
cells is one measurement presented as many, and a model cannot tell nine
readings that agree from one reading copied nine times. A fine value averaged
into a coarse cell discards exactly what the finer source was bought for. And
distance is a feature that a grid hides: from the same five nearest Argo
profiles, optimal interpolation beat climatology by 21\,\% on temperature where
the gridded monthly product, read at the nearest cell, gave a learned codec
3\,\% (E-076b).

\section{The token, and the two footprint fields}

Every observation, from every tier, becomes one token:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{token} = \big(\,\mathbf{v}[C],\ \mathbf{m}[C],\ \Delta x,\ \Delta y,\ \Delta t,\ \log_2 f_p,\ \log_2 f_t,\ n_R,\ s,\ c\,\big)
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{v}$ is the measurement with mask $\mathbf{m}$ marking which of
its $C$ components were measured; $(\Delta x, \Delta y)$ the offset from the
anchor in kilometres east and north and $\Delta t$ the age in days, measured
back from the \emph{end} of the anchor's bin; $n_R$ the number of observations
inside the search bounds; and $s$, $c$ learned embeddings of the source class
and the channel. The two footprint fields are
\begin{equation}
\log_2 f_p = \log_2\!\frac{\text{footprint in km}}{27.83\,\text{km}}, \qquad
\log_2 f_t = \log_2\!\frac{\text{support in days}}{5\,\text{days}},
\end{equation}
the spatial and temporal support in units of one $0.25^\circ$ cell
($27.83$\,km, being $0.25^\circ$ of latitude at 111.32\,km per degree) and one
pentad, clamped to $\pm 12$. They carry what the offsets cannot: a value 30\,km
from a $1.9^\circ$ reanalysis average and one 30\,km from a point measurement
say different things about the anchor, because the coarse value is a smoother,
lower-variance quantity.

nine arrays in \code{.npy} format and one \code{store.json}. Every store has
exactly these files, with exactly these dtypes, whatever the source.

\paragraph{The bin, and why it may be negative.} A bin is a five-day period
counted from 1982-01-01, so
$\mathrm{bin} = \lfloor (\text{date} - \text{1982-01-01}) / 5\,\text{d}
\rfloor$. Drifters begin in 1979 and the carbon archive in 1957, and those rows
are \textbf{kept, with negative bins}, so the store is the whole record and the
\emph{loader} clips to whatever axis a tensor has --- truncating at build time
cannot be undone. The reader's definition is a true floor on floats, which is
the whole reason the negative side works: \code{np.floor(days / 5)} puts $-0.5$
days in bin $-1$, where integer division would put it in bin 0.

\paragraph{The index.} \code{bin\_offsets} is a compressed-sparse-row index:
the rows of bin $b$ are \code{off[b - bin\_first] : off[b - bin\_first + 1]},
with \code{off[0] == 0} and \code{off[-1] == N}. \textbf{\code{bin\_first} is
not zero} --- it is $-1768$ for \code{socat} and $+803$ for \code{slatrack} ---
and code that indexes by \code{bin} without subtracting it will either crash
or, worse, wrap.

\paragraph{The sort order is part of the contract.} Rows are sorted by
\code{(bin, time\_days)} ascending. The bin order is what makes the index valid
at all; the within-bin time order is what lets a reader stop at the anchor's own
bin and still have the most recent observations at the end of the slice.

\paragraph{The longitude invariant, and the float32-cast lesson.} \code{lon}
is in $[-180, 180)$, half-open, and the dateline is not a wall: the reader wraps
the longitude difference, and a consumer computing offsets by hand must too.
Enforcing the half-open interval cost two fixes on the same day, and they are
one lesson. The first (\code{61ab4fa}) folds a source sample at exactly
$+180.0^\circ$\,E back into the interval; the second (\code{fb5d5ab}) moves the
wrap to \emph{after} the float32 cast, because a source longitude of
$179.99999$ wraps to itself in float64 and \textbf{rounds up to $180.0$} when it
is stored in float32 --- which the build's own assertion then, rightly, refused.
\textbf{An invariant has to hold in the column's dtype, not in the wider type it
was computed in.} Both fixes are visible in the published bytes: over
\code{slatrack}'s 2.03 billion rows the maximum longitude is $179.99998474$ ---
\code{nextafter(180, 0)} in float32 --- the minimum is exactly $-180.0$, and not
one row sits at $+180.0$.

\paragraph{The float32 timestamp.} \code{time\_days} is float32 and runs to
$\approx 15{,}700$ days, where its spacing is $2^{-10}\,\mathrm{d} = 84.4$
seconds. The builder therefore computes \code{bin} from the float32 value that
is \emph{actually stored}, not from the float64 the parser had: otherwise a row
would sit in bin $b$ carrying a timestamp that reads as bin $b+1$, and the
search --- which requires $\Delta t = 5(b+1) - t \ge 0$ --- would silently never
return it for its own anchor. The identity \code{bin == floor(time\_days / 5)}
holds on every row of all four stores. Section~6.4 says what the 84 seconds
cost.

\paragraph{Raw units, on purpose.} Nothing is z-scored and nothing is
anomalised: a measurement already divided by a training-set standard deviation
is no longer one. Standardise in the loader, from training bins only --- a
statistic computed over the whole store carries the test years into the weights,
and that leak is invisible.

\section{The four stores}

All four were built on 2026-09-14 and then \textbf{independently verified the
same day by a session that did not build them}: every file fetched from the Hub
and hashed against \code{store.json}'s own record, then every structural
property and every statistic \emph{recomputed from the arrays} rather than read
out of the metadata claiming them
(\code{docs/FAMILY10\_VERIFICATION\_2026-09-14.md}). Physical bounds are shared:
$|u|, |v| < 5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$; $-3 < \mathrm{SST} < 45\,^\circ$C;
$0 < f\mathrm{CO_2} < 2000\,\mu$atm; $|\mathrm{sla}| < 3$\,m;
$0 \le \mathrm{sss} \le 45$\,PSU; $|\mathrm{wind}| < 120\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$;
$-60 < \mathrm{airt} < 60\,^\circ$C. A value outside is set to \textbf{NaN and
counted}, never clipped --- clipping puts a fabricated number where a broken
instrument was.

\subsection{January to March 1994 is one satellite}

1994 is the only year that falls rather than rises: 29,146,039 rows against
$\approx 33$\,M either side, a deficit of 3,835,024, and per-mission
\emph{monthly} counts locate it exactly. ERS-1's 35-day repeat (\code{e1}) stops
in mid-December 1993 --- 1993-12 is itself a half month, 699,535 rows against a
1.31\,M median --- and does not resume until mid-March 1995, while its geodetic
phase (\code{e1g}) starts 1994-04-10, exactly its STAC window's first day. So
the single-satellite interval is \textbf{1993-12-15 $\to$ 1994-04-09}, and
January, February and March 1994 carry \textbf{TOPEX/Poseidon alone} --- 1.59,
1.47 and 1.50\,M rows against 2.94, 2.70 and 2.96\,M a year earlier. With
April's partial start those four months are the whole deficit, and the record's
thinnest bin, 152,741 rows of 2,339, sits inside it. DUACS publishes no level-3
product for the 3-day ice-phase orbit ERS-1 flew in between, which is why the
gap is a hole in \code{e1} rather than a thinner \code{e1}.
\textbf{Nothing is missing from the build; the constellation was.}

\subsection{GFO's empty months}

Scanning every mission for a calendar month lying wholly inside its own STAC
window and carrying \emph{zero} rows turns up two, both GFO: \textbf{2004-03}
and \textbf{2006-09}, against a 1,435,239-row median month. Two more GFO months
are far below that median without being empty (2003-09 at 257,212; 2007-02 at
161,740), and from 2007-02 the mission never again reaches 1.1\,M in a month:
the shape of a satellite degrading. It is flagged rather than passed over
silently because this check cannot distinguish ``the archive holds no file''
from ``the file was not asked for'' from the outside; what it \emph{can} say is
that the fetch lane refuses to mark a year in which a listing came back empty or
a download came back short, and all of 2000--2008 are marked. One other
mission-month is comparable (\code{h2b} 2021-08, 272,078 against a 1,291,653
median). Treat GFO-era coverage as uneven.

\subsection{The time column is coarser than the sampling, by 84$\times$}

\code{time\_days} is float32. By 2015 the integer part needs 14 bits, leaving
10 for the fraction, so the column resolves $2^{-10}\,\mathrm{d} = 84.4$
seconds --- and the instrument samples \textbf{once a second}.

So 21 to 84 consecutive samples of one mission carry the identical timestamp,
and at 6.5\,km between samples that is 130\,km of track in 1993 and

rather than errors. The search's $\Delta t$ \textbf{cannot order two samples
less than 84 seconds apart}: every token from one overflight arrives with the
same age and the tie-break falls entirely to distance. The store is nonetheless
correctly sorted --- \code{time\_days} is non-decreasing inside every one of the
2,339 bins, checked row by row --- because the assembler's stable sort preserved
the parts' own order inside each run of equal timestamps. And \code{bin} is
exact, so \textbf{a calendar date must be derived from \code{bin}}. This is
\code{socat}'s float32 finding one order of magnitude larger: \code{socat} loses
84 seconds against measurements minutes apart, \code{slatrack} against
measurements one second apart. Nothing is hidden --- the \code{schema} declares
float32 --- but this is the first store whose time column is coarser than its own
sampling interval.

\section{The build, and its guards}

One builder, \code{ml/build\_family10\_stores.py}, with a source adapter per
store and three stages: \code{index} lists the archive and writes the plan of
files or years; \code{fetch} streams year by year and appends to per-year column
parts; \code{publish} concatenates the parts in bin order, writes the index,
computes hashes, uploads, and then \textbf{downloads each file back and
re-hashes it} --- a publish that cannot verify fails the job. Every one of the
four build logs ends with \code{publish: 10 file(s) verified by restore}.

\paragraph{Three stores on a free hosted runner.} \code{gdp}, \code{gtmba} and
\code{socat} are keyless and each fits a hosted runner's six-hour cap and
14\,GB of disk, so all three cost \textbf{\$0}: about 20 minutes each for
\code{gtmba} and \code{socat}, 85 for \code{gdp} --- and the 85 is the source,
not the builder, because the drifter server serves one month per request and 46
years is about 550 sequential ones.

\paragraph{\code{slatrack} is built on two machines, because no one machine may
be both.} The Copernicus credentials may not live on a rented box, and a hosted
runner cannot hold 67\,GB. The seam is a prefix on the Hub itself. Six hosted
lanes over weighted year ranges each fetch one year, push that year's column
parts to \code{partials/family10/slatrack/<year>/}, delete the local copy and
move on --- with \code{done.json} written \textbf{last}, and only after every
file has been downloaded back with a matching hash, so a marker can only
under-claim and a year killed halfway is simply refetched. A schedule every six
hours is the resume mechanism. A keyless box then pulls every year back,
verifies each file against its \code{done.json}, refuses by name if any year is
missing, and assembles.

\paragraph{The streaming assembler.} 2.03 billion rows do not fit in memory
beside their own sort, so the store is assembled in three passes over the
parts: count rows per bin to get the CSR offsets;
scatter each part's rows into \code{offsets[bin] + cursor[bin]} through a
memory-mapped writer, in the order the parts are read; then sort each bin's
slice by \code{time\_days} with a \emph{stable} argsort. That is the same
permutation the in-memory assembler's \code{np.lexsort((time\_days, bin))}
produces, because lexsort is stable and its final tie-break is input order ---
which the scatter reproduces. A test hashes both assemblers' output over a
synthetic archive doctored to hold duplicate \code{(bin, time\_days)} rows
across two parts and asserts they are \textbf{byte-identical}. Peak memory is
one part plus one bin's slice, and a disk preflight computes the store's size
from the dtypes and refuses before pass 2 if free space is under $1.2\times$ it.

\paragraph{The assertions a store must pass before it is trusted.}
\code{check\_store} runs on every assembled store and again before any publish:
rows sorted by \code{(bin, time\_days)}; \code{bin} equal to
\code{floor(time\_days / 5)}; the CSR offsets spanning exactly the rows, each

$|\mathrm{lat}| \le 90$; no infinity anywhere in \code{values}; the footprint
columns constant; every channel inside its declared bounds; and a
nearest-neighbour search returning nothing from the future. Each is a property a
broken build can have while looking entirely ordinary from outside, which is the
only reason they are worth the seconds. Above 50\,M part rows the check runs
chunked, which is why it fits at all.

\paragraph{Three guards worth naming, because each closed a silent failure.}
\code{gtmba}'s build records \code{datasets\_empty: 37} --- request-and-year
combinations that came back with nothing --- because a silent skip of seventeen
mooring sites is invisible in a store that otherwise looks complete. The
\code{slatrack} assembler refuses, \emph{by name}, if any requested year is
absent from the Hub prefix, so a partial store is only ever built deliberately,
with \code{--allow-missing-years}. And a store built under any relaxation
records it in a \code{degraded} block, so a consumer can tell a complete build
from a permitted-partial one without reading a workflow log.

\paragraph{Two ledger bugs, found by arithmetic and fixed with the arrays
untouched.} \code{socat}'s first build attached the whole stream's counters to
every year part and the assembler summed them across the 59 years that held
rows, so \code{rows\_read} read 2,597,074,036 instead of 44,018,204 --- exactly

count in the channel it describes, and the row ledger failed to close by the
same 568: one cause --- 568 source fixes on which \emph{nothing at all} was
measured, counted and then discarded unrecorded --- and two symptoms. Both stores
were rebuilt the same day, and in both cases \textbf{all nine array sha256
values in the new \code{store.json} are byte-identical to the verified build's},
so only the ledger changed and no measurement had to be re-checked. Both ledgers
now close: \code{socat} reads 44,018,204 rows with 2,160,005 and 27,520 dropped,
and \code{gdp} $48{,}708{,}540 - 227{,}174 - 568 - 48{,}480{,}798 = 0$.

\section{Reading a store}

\code{ml/family10\_store.py} is the only thing in the repository that opens a
store, so the layout is defined once. It is pure numpy: no scipy, no torch.

\begin{verbatim}
from family10_store import Store

st  = Store.open("tensors/family10/gdp")                        # a directory
st  = Store.open("chfrank/earth-tensors:tensors/family10/gdp")  # or the Hub
tok = st.knearest(36.0, -70.0, bin=2411, k=8,
                  R_max_km=300.0, T_max_days=10.0)
\end{verbatim}

\code{Store.open} takes a directory or a Hub prefix; the Hub form downloads
\code{store.json} first and then exactly the files it names, never a whole-repo
snapshot. \code{verify\_store(dir)} re-hashes every file against
\code{store.json}'s own record and raises on any mismatch --- worth running,
because a truncated \code{values.npy} still memory-maps and still answers a
search, with whatever its tail happens to hold.

\code{knearest} returns a dict of \textbf{fixed length $k$} --- always $k$
slots, so a batch is never ragged: \code{values} $(k, C)$ float32 in raw units
with \code{mask} $(k, C)$ beside it; \code{dx\_km} and \code{dy\_km}, signed
kilometres east and north \emph{from the anchor}, so the model never has to
learn the cosine of latitude; \code{dt\_days}, always $\ge 0$; \code{dist\_km}
and \code{d2}; \code{log2\_fp} and \code{log2\_dt} per returned row;
\code{platform} and \code{qc}; \code{n\_R}, the scalar count of observations
inside the bounds --- the local-density feature; \code{valid}, False marking a
\textbf{miss token} (NaN values, \code{row = -1}); and \code{row}, \code{lat},
\code{lon}, \code{time\_days}, \code{n\_found}, \code{channels}.

Three rules are structural rather than applied afterwards. \textbf{One-sided in
time}: only observations at or before the anchor's pentad are candidates,
because the CSR slice stops at the anchor's own bin and never reads a later row
--- a forecaster that has seen the next pentad is not a forecaster.
\textbf{Bounded} by \code{R\_max\_km} and \code{T\_max\_days}; where fewer than
$k$ exist inside them the remaining slots are miss tokens, the same token the
gridded tensor uses at an empty cell, made rare instead of usual. And the
\textbf{metric} is $d^2 = (dx^2 + dy^2)/L^2 + dt^2/T^2$ with $L$ and $T$
defaulting to the search bounds, which makes the two axes commensurate at the
boundary; pass them explicitly to use a channel's own correlation length.
$\Delta t$ is measured from the \emph{end} of the anchor's pentad,
$5(\mathrm{bin}+1)$ days after the epoch, so an observation inside the anchor's
own bin is 0 to 5 days old rather than arriving from the future. \code{qc\_max}
narrows to the source's best grade \emph{before} \code{n\_R} is counted: a token
the reader will not return must not inflate the density feature.

\paragraph{A worked example.} At $36^\circ$\,N, $70^\circ$\,W in bin 2411
with $k = 8$, $R_{\max} = 300$\,km, $T_{\max} = 10$\,d, \code{gdp} returns
eight rows in 4.3\,ms from \textbf{one} drogued drifter (identifier 116295) at
exact 0.25-day steps, walking east over two days at 0.31--0.54\,m\,s$^{-1}$
with \code{sst} NaN throughout because that buoy's thermistor is not reporting.
\code{gtmba} returns eight miss tokens there in 0.2\,ms, correctly --- its array
spans latitudes $[-25, 21]$ only --- while at the TAO site $0^\circ$\,N,
$140^\circ$\,W it returns the $0/+2/-2^\circ$ triplet at exactly
$222.64 = 2 \times 111.32$\,km spacing and a monotone thermocline from
$26\,^\circ$C to $11.6\,^\circ$C, where \code{gdp} returns eight miss tokens.
\textbf{The two stores are complementary at that anchor, which is the point of
the family.} Typical cost: \code{gtmba} 0.4\,ms, \code{gdp} 4.3\,ms,
\code{socat} 11\,ms.

\paragraph{Family 8's Argo store opens through the same class, unchanged.}
It reads that store's \code{temp} and \code{psal} blocks as one 32-column value
matrix --- temperature at the 16 Roemmich--Gilson pressures, then salinity at
the same 16 --- and takes the footprint constants out of its own
\code{store.json}. The blocks stay separate memory maps and only the $k$
selected rows are ever concatenated, so opening the 2.68\,M-profile store costs
nothing. The test suite asserts the result is \textbf{bit-identical} to the
family-8 reader's on every anchor it tries.

\section{Limitations, and what is still open}

\paragraph{The $k$ tokens are usually one platform.} Measured over 600
on-track anchors per store, the number of \emph{distinct} platforms among the
$k$ returned observations has a median of \textbf{1 of 8} for \code{gdp},
\textbf{1 of 8} for \code{socat} and \textbf{2 of 4} for \code{gtmba}: the
eight drifter slots are one buoy at six-hour intervals, the eight carbon slots
one research cruise seconds apart along one line, and raising the radius does
not change it. So $k$ buys \emph{one track's redundancy} --- genuine information
about that track's coherence, and not $k$ nearby measurements of the ocean.
Whether the search should be de-duplicated by \code{platform}, or $k$ made much
larger than 8, or neither, is \textbf{E-078b's open question}: the answer
depends on what the token is for, and nothing measured so far measures that.
\code{slatrack} will be the extreme case --- at 6.5\,km between consecutive
samples, any $k$ under a few hundred is one overflight.

\paragraph{The suggested search radii were three to ten times too loose.} At anchors drawn from each store's own rows the $k$-th nearest observation sits far closer than the design assumed.

\code{gtmba}'s 4th neighbour is at 0\,km at the median because the array is a
fixed lattice and the same mooring contributes several daily rows inside the
window --- the temporal bound, not the spatial one, is what $k = 4$ actually
spends. \code{socat}'s 30-day window is the one bound worth leaving generous: a
ship revisiting a piece of ocean is rare, so a longer window is the only way a
\emph{second} cruise can ever enter the search.

\paragraph{Off-track, the miss token is the normal answer.} At anchors drawn
uniformly over the globe, the search returns \textbf{nothing at all} inside the
suggested bounds on \textbf{71\,\%} of anchors for \code{gdp},
\textbf{81\,\%} for \code{gtmba} and \textbf{89\,\%} for \code{socat}, and
widening $R$ only makes the search slower. \code{mask} and \code{n\_R} are
\emph{inputs}, not defects: $n_R = 0$ is a true statement about the observing
system, and exactly what a gridded representation could not say. Do not impute,
and do not fill NaN with zero --- zero is a real fugacity and a real velocity.

\paragraph{Tier T is not built.} The tile tier --- a tile-date catalogue plus a
small per-tile codec's cached tokens, held as a tier-P store --- is designed and
has no bytes. It is the one compute-bound step on the E-078 ladder, and it is
argued on its own evidence rather than on this note's.

\paragraph{\code{values} is float16.} About three decimal digits: $\approx
0.002\,^\circ$C at $20\,^\circ$C and $\approx 0.5\,\mu$atm at
$700\,\mu$atm --- below every instrument's own uncertainty, and it halves the
store. Cast to float32 before arithmetic; the reader already does.

\paragraph{Two provenance strings in \code{slatrack}'s \code{store.json} are
stale.} Its \code{verified} field still ends ``Neither route can be run from
this sandbox \ldots\ NOT YET MEASURED: the remote path layout of the original
files'', and its \code{sources} names the \code{copernicusmarine subset} call.
Both were written before any fetch had succeeded; the build that produced the
store ran the \code{copernicusmarine.get}-on-original-files route end to end
over 32 years. The product and the dataset identifiers are right, the call named
is the one that was measured and rejected. Both are class constants, both are
one-line fixes, and \textbf{no byte of any array is affected}.

\paragraph{The holdout protocol is unchanged.} 2009, 2017 and 2023 are
development years; train through 2020, test 2021--2024. The stores carry the
\emph{whole} record, pre-1982 included, and the loader restricts --- which is why
the negative bins are kept rather than dropped at build time.

\paragraph{Verify before you trust.} Four checks, in order of cost.
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt
\item \textbf{sha256} of every array equals \code{store.json}'s own record, and \code{store.json}'s equals the table above.
\item \textbf{$N$ from the file size.} Each array must satisfy
$\mathrm{bytes} = 128 + N \times \mathrm{itemsize} \times \mathrm{cols}$ with
$N$ equal to \code{store.json}'s \code{N}, and \code{bin\_offsets.npy} must
hold $\text{\code{n\_bins}} + 1$ int64 entries. The \code{.npy} header declares
its own dtype and shape; parse it and compare against \code{schema}.
\item \textbf{\code{bin} is monotonic} and never leaves $[\text{\code{bin\_first}}, \text{\code{bin\_last}}]$; \code{time\_days} is non-decreasing inside each bin; and \code{bin == floor(time\_days / 5)} on every row.
\item \textbf{The index is consistent}: \code{off[0]} is 0, \code{off[-1]} is
$N$, the array is non-decreasing, and the cumulative sum of
\code{bincount(bin - bin\_first)} reproduces it exactly --- equivalently, every
row of the slice \code{off[i]:off[i+1]} has \code{bin == bin\_first + i}.
\end{enumerate}
All four were run on all four stores on 2026-09-14 and all four passed. If a
store is too large to hold --- \code{slatrack} is 67\,GB --- stream it: read the
nine files over HTTPS in row-aligned lockstep, one connection per file, hashing
each as its bytes arrive while one consumer recomputes the checks on the chunk
in flight. That is how \code{slatrack} was verified, in one pass at 80\,MB/s
using 1.6\,GB of memory; eight connections were dropped mid-stream by the
content-delivery network and each was resumed by \code{Range} from the byte it
had reached, which changes nothing, because the hash is over the byte sequence.

\paragraph{A minimal reader, in numpy alone,} depending on nothing from the source repository:

\begin{verbatim}
import json, numpy as np

D = "tensors/family10/gdp"                 # a local copy of one store
meta = json.load(open(f"{D}/store.json"))
A = {n: np.load(f"{D}/{n}.npy", mmap_mode="r") for n in
     ("bin", "time_days", "lat", "lon", "values", "platform", "qc", "fp")}
off       = np.load(f"{D}/bin_offsets.npy")        # int64 [B+1], read whole
bin_first = meta["bin_first"]
channels  = [c["name"] for c in meta["channels"]]

def bin_of(date):                          # "2015-01-03" -> 2411
    d = (np.datetime64(date) - np.datetime64("1982-01-01")).astype("int64")
    return int(np.floor(d / 5.0))          # floor, so pre-1982 is negative

def rows_of_bin(b):                        # the CSR slice; may be empty
    i = b - bin_first
    return (slice(int(off[i]), int(off[i + 1]))
            if 0 <= i < len(off) - 1 else slice(0, 0))

def knearest(lat0, lon0, b, k=8, R_max_km=110.0, T_max_days=4.0):
    """The k nearest observations at or BEFORE bin b.  One-sided by
    construction: the scan stops at bin b.  dt runs from the END of bin b,
    so a row inside b is 0..5 days old, never negative."""
    KM, t_anchor = 111.32, 5.0 * (b + 1)
    b_lo = b - int(np.ceil(T_max_days / 5.0)) - 1     # bins the bound admits
    lo = rows_of_bin(max(b_lo, bin_first)).start
    hi = rows_of_bin(b).stop
    if hi <= lo:
        return None
    lat = np.asarray(A["lat"][lo:hi], np.float64)
    lon = np.asarray(A["lon"][lo:hi], np.float64)
    dt  = t_anchor - np.asarray(A["time_days"][lo:hi], np.float64)
    dy  = (lat - lat0) * KM
    dlon = np.mod(lon - lon0 + 180.0, 360.0) - 180.0  # the dateline is no wall
    dx  = dlon * KM * np.maximum(np.cos(np.radians(0.5 * (lat + lat0))), 0.05)
    dist = np.hypot(dx, dy)
    ok = (dist <= R_max_km) & (dt >= 0.0) & (dt <= T_max_days)
    n_R = int(ok.sum())                    # the local-density feature
    idx = np.flatnonzero(ok)
    if idx.size == 0:
        return {"n_R": 0, "valid": np.zeros(k, bool)}   # k miss tokens
    d2 = ((dx[idx]**2 + dy[idx]**2) / R_max_km**2
          + dt[idx]**2 / T_max_days**2)
    idx = idx[np.argsort(d2, kind="stable")[:k]]
    rows = lo + idx
    v = np.asarray(A["values"][rows], np.float32)       # float16 -> float32
    fp = np.asarray(A["fp"][rows], np.float32)
    return {"values": v, "mask": np.isfinite(v), "channels": channels,
            "dx_km": dx[idx], "dy_km": dy[idx], "dt_days": dt[idx],
            "dist_km": dist[idx], "n_R": n_R,
            "log2_fp": fp[:, 0], "log2_dt": fp[:, 1],
            "platform": np.asarray(A["platform"][rows]),
            "qc": np.asarray(A["qc"][rows]),
            "valid": np.ones(len(rows), bool)}

tok = knearest(36.0, -70.0, bin_of("2015-01-03"))
\end{verbatim}

Four things this code does that a naive version does not, each load-bearing.
It floors on \emph{floats}, so a date before 1982 lands in a negative bin rather
than bin 0. It subtracts \code{bin\_first} before indexing the index array. It
wraps the longitude difference into $[-180, 180)$, so an anchor near the
dateline still finds its neighbours. And it measures $\Delta t$ from the end of
the anchor's bin, so a row inside the anchor's own pentad reads as 0 to 5 days
old rather than as arriving from the future. Pad to $k$ slots with miss tokens
if you want rectangular batches, and standardise from your training bins only.

\paragraph{How tier G points at family 7.2.} The four tier-G groups are not
stored under \code{tensors/family10/}. The registry's \code{tier\_g} block names
the tensor by stem --- \code{family7\_global025\_pentad\_l2}, recipe \code{f7l2}
--- and gives its manifest URL; each group entry carries \code{prefix}
\code{tensors/family7\_global025\_pentad\_l2}, the file name, its byte count and
sha256, plus \code{grid}, \code{shape}, \code{dtype}, \code{header\_len},
\code{slab\_bytes} and \code{bin\_first}. A read is one HTTP range: for a
bin-major array, $\mathrm{offset} = \text{\code{header\_len}} +
(b - \text{\code{bin\_first}}) \times \text{\code{slab\_bytes}}$ and length
\code{slab\_bytes}. The response must be HTTP 206; a 200 means the host ignored
the \code{Range} header and is about to send tens of gigabytes, and should be
refused rather than consumed. The values are float16 and \emph{z-scored}, so
un-standardise them with the group's \code{norm} out of the tensor's
\code{.npz}; the full account of that tensor is the family 7.2 design note,
\code{ml/paper/notes/family72.pdf}. Verify tier-G files against
\code{tensors/family7\_global025\_pentad\_l2/manifest.json}, which is their
authority; the registry's copies were checked against it and matched 4 of 4.

\paragraph{Deprecation.} \code{family7\_global025\_pentad\_l1} will be
\textbf{deleted from the Hugging Face dataset repository on 2026-10-15}. It is
superseded in every respect by \code{f7l2}, whose three inherited files are the
same bytes, and nothing in it is unique. Nothing in family 10 references it ---
\code{tier\_g} names \code{f7l2} --- so if your ingest points at \code{l1},
repoint it before that date. \code{family7\_global025\_pentad\_l0} stays as the
published original, because its hashes are cited elsewhere, but it carries a
missing year of observed sea-surface temperature and sea ice and a
non-reproducing atmosphere group, so it is a historical record rather than a
recommendation. Pin \code{f7l2} by its hashes and re-read the manifest before a
re-ingest.

sha256, the bin or row range, and the check that failed. If issues are
